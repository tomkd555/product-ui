# Which traits make generated web screens read as AI-made, why they repel readers, and which of them a check can catch

As of 2026-10-30. Tier: DEEP.

## Answer

Designers, agencies and tool authors who wrote about AI-generated web pages in 2025 and 2026 name a recurring first set of default traits, and a second set appears once these are banned [E10]. The first set is a purple or indigo gradient running to blue or pink, an unchosen default typeface such as Inter, one large radius and one soft shadow on every card, a row of 3 equal feature cards with emoji or icon tiles, a centred hero with a pill badge in a fixed section order, and generic benefit copy with invented proof [E1, E3, E5, E13, E33, E116, E120]. Every source that names these traits is a blog, an agency, a vendor or a single-author rule set, and no study found in this run measures how readers react to these UI traits one by one.

The harm has grade A evidence one level up, at the point where a reader attributes the work to AI. Labelling or disclosing AI authorship lowers liking, trust and perceived effort [E70, E71, E72, E73], and consumers forgive misrepresentation in AI-made ads less because those ads look low-effort [E74]. When authorship is hidden, readers often cannot tell AI output apart and sometimes prefer it [E71, E75], and blind raters judged AI-generated prototypes usable but unoriginal [E93]. Fabricated content and broken behaviour do harm with no attribution at all: errors and technical problems cost a site credibility [E69, E92], and humans detect fake reviews no better than chance [E83]. The distaste is therefore very likely the reader concluding that no decision was made and no effort was spent, plus, where proof is invented, a sense of being deceived (lead's inference from [E73, E74]). The measured uncanny-valley effect concerns human-looking entities [E82], and no study in this run applies it to page layouts.

For the decision, the traits split into four classes. Colour and gradient values, typefaces, radius and shadow uniformity, emoji icons, glass and motion utilities and stock vocabulary have signals that the sources tie to code [E5, E13, E15]. A pattern can also find negative parallelism, em-dashes and figures in the copy, while whether a figure has a source is settled against the brief (lead's inference). Rows of equal tiles, the shape of the hero and the radius actually rendered need the DOM in a browser. One author puts the first view, the visible proof and the navigation ahead of colour and radius [E114], and well-designed empty states help users start their tasks [E88]. Settling these, with an inventory of empty, loading and error states, in a step before markup is the lead's inference. Headline genericness has no pattern signal beyond fixed templates such as "for modern": Landing Doctor's test is whether the headline still reads true with a competitor's logo swapped in [E34]. This run assigns that judgement, with whole-page sameness, to an independent reviewer (lead's inference).

Two limits shape any rule set. Once the first defaults are banned, models fall back to a second set [E10, E96]. In arXiv abstracts, "delve" and other words flagged as ChatGPT-favoured dropped after they were publicised while others kept rising [E42], so a fixed ban list drifts out of date (lead's inference). In one study of web pages, prototypicality correlated strongly with perceived trustworthiness [E68]. From this the lead infers that the rules should target unchosen defaults and fabricated content and leave conventional structure in place. A controlled study showing that removing the visual traits, with content held constant, leaves trust and attribution unchanged would change this answer.

## Findings

### 1. Visual traits that readers name, and the signal of each

The most named visual trait is a purple or indigo gradient. In this run at least 10 publishers name it [E1, E3, E5, E12, E13, E35, E95, E104, E107, E117], and a verifier found it on at least 13 of the pages the collectors saved, so 10 is a lower bound. CodeMySpec builds its list on 2 earlier inventories, which it calls independent [E13]; whether the other publishers copied each other was not checked. The signals they give are concrete: Tailwind's `bg-indigo-500`, which prg.sh traces to the buttons of Tailwind UI [E1, E2]; `indigo-600` with `slate-900` [E15]; an indigo-to-purple gradient [E3]; purple fading to blue "often on the headline text itself" [E5]; and a hero background running from violet `#7c3aed` to pink `#ec4899`, or from navy to light blue [E117]. 925studios calls the blue-to-purple gradient "the single loudest AI tell in 2026" [E4]. The colour comes from the model's habits: shadcn/ui's default primary is a neutral `oklch(0.205 0 0)`, and Tailwind assigns no primary colour at all [E16].

An unchosen typeface comes second. 7 publishers name Inter, Roboto, Arial or the system stack as the default face [E1, E3, E5, E13, E95, E104, E108], and 4 of them describe one face used for every word [E3, E5, E104, E108]. Anthropic's cookbook prompt lists "Inter, Roboto, Arial, system fonts" and adds that the model still converges "on common choices (Space Grotesk, for example) across generations" [E95, E96]. The anti-ai-slop skill adds Geist to the list [E108]. 925studios states the reason: "Inter unchosen signals nobody made a typography decision" [E4].

The "card kit" is the third group. 3 publishers describe both one radius on every element and the same shadow under every card [E7, E13, E100], and 3 more name the uniform or large radius alone or on feature cards [E11, E108, E116]. The named signals are `rounded-2xl` and `rounded-3xl`, `shadow-lg` and `p-6` [E15], a shadow of `rgba(0,0,0,.1)` [E100], and a radius near 16px with a faint shadow [E116]. Noqta gives the reason: "When every element has identical corners, hierarchy disappears" [E11].

A row of 3 feature cards is named by 7 publishers [E1, E3, E6, E13, E35, E115, E116], and 4 of them say the cards are equal or identical [E3, E13, E35, E116]. Superdesign describes it as "Always three, always an emoji or a thin-line icon, always a two-word title and a lorem-ish sentence" [E36]. A Japanese freelancer who has delivered more than 50 landing pages writes that the feature section of AI-made pages takes this shape 「ほぼ100%」, with rockets or gears as emoji on cards of identical width [E116].

The fixed page order is named by 5 publishers [E6, E13, E36, E112, E115]. Capital and Compute lists it as "Centred hero, pill badge, two buttons, logo row, three feature cards, pricing, CTA" [E6]. A pill badge in the hero appears in 2 of these, and CodeMySpec places it directly above the headline [E6, E13]. A Japanese note article calls 「均質化」 and 「テンプレート感」 the most prominent trait of AI-made sites, because the same order leaves readers thinking 「またこの構成か」 [E112].

Fewer sources name the remaining visual traits:

- glass panels with `backdrop-blur` and glow with no purpose [E8, E13];
- motion on everything: the same fade-and-slide-up on each section, hover transitions on every card, count-up statistics [E8, E98, E109];
- template chrome: a tracked-out uppercase eyebrow above each heading, meta strings joined with middle dots, "WORD — fragment" labels and an arrow appended to links [E12, E14, E100];
- emoji used as icons in navigation and feature lists [E14, E36, E108, E116];
- a "bento" layout that chops a simple page into evenly weighted tiles [E17].

aismells adds that the bento grid is a mainstream design trend of 2024 to 2026, so the trait is over-application of a real pattern [E18]; another page the collectors saved recommends bento grids as a fix.

A second set of defaults has already appeared. Capital and Compute reports that once purple and Inter are banned, coding agents produce "warm cream with a terracotta accent, or near-black with one acid-green highlight" [E10]. Anthropic's frontend-design skill now lists these looks as traits of AI-made design: a cream background near `#F4F1EA` with a serif display and a terracotta accent near `#D97757`, a near-black ground with one acid-green or vermilion accent, and a broadsheet layout with hairline rules and zero radius [E99]. Impeccable's README describes the unguided output of one model as "cream page, thin serif, italic accent, pill buttons" [E104].

The sources agree on what a visual trait proves. The avoid-ai-design documentation, quoted by Capital and Compute, says a trait "is evidence of a default nobody chose, never proof that a model made the page" [E9]. 925studios writes that "None of these choices are bad on their own" [E3].

### 2. Copy traits in English and Japanese

Stock benefit vocabulary is named by 5 publishers [E8, E14, E22, E32, E109]. The words they list are "Empower, Unlock, Transform" with two-noun feature names such as "Seamless Integration" [E14]; "powerful, seamless, AI-driven, end-to-end, robust, intuitive" [E32]; "Elevate your workflow" [E8]; and "Elevate", "Unlock", "Seamlessly" [E109]. The academic work measures over-representation in scientific prose. An excess-word analysis of PubMed abstracts implies that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs [E38]. Between 2022 and 2024, "delve" rose 1,500%, "underscore" 1,000% and "intricate" 700% across scholarly databases [E39]. Of 135 candidate terms, 103 rose meaningfully in 2024 [E41]. No study in this run measures these words in UI or marketing copy, so the marketing list rests on practitioners' reports.

Co-occurrence carries more signal than any one word. In 2024 "underscore" correlated with "pivotal" at 0.449, against 0.032 in 2022 [E40]. Wikipedia's AI-cleanup guide treats one or two such words as possibly coincidental and an edit with many of them, many times, as "one of the strongest tells" [E19]. The lists age: the use of "delve" dropped soon after it was publicised in early 2024 [E42], and Wikipedia's list for mid-2026 models has moved to words such as "leverage", "foster" and "dependable" [E21]. A preprint finds no evidence that model architecture or training data causes the over-representation and names human-feedback tuning as a possible cause [E43].

Headline formulas are named by 4 publishers, 2 of them Japanese [E34, E35, E115, E120]. Superdesign's first tell is the "mad-lib headline", "The AI-powered [X] for modern [Y]" [E35]. Landing Doctor names "The modern platform for teams" and "Streamline your workflow", and gives a test: "Swap in a competitor's logo and it still reads true" [E34]. The Japanese examples are 「未来の働き方を変える」 and 「あなたの可能性を解き放つ」 [E115], 「あなたの課題を解決」 and 「最適なソリューション」 [E120], and 「手間なく集客を実現」 [E121].

The rule of three is named by Wikipedia's guide, from "adjective, adjective, adjective" to "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase" [E23]. Superdesign gives the landing-page form, "Save time. Work smarter. Scale faster." [E36]. Negative parallelism, "It's not X, it's Y", is called "The single most commonly identified AI writing tell" by the single author of tropes.fyi, with no measurement behind the claim [E28]. Barron's counted the construction in 208 documents of large US companies in 2025, against 100 in 2024 and 49 in 2023 [E30]. The same report quotes a detection company calling it "no smoking gun for AI use", and Schwab said its flagged quote was "100% human-generated" [E31].

The em-dash has the best measurement among the punctuation traits. Its prevalence in medRxiv Discussion sections rose from 4.23% before ChatGPT to 11.58% after it and to 20.3% in 2025, and the author calls this a population-level indicator [E44]. Wikipedia's guide says LLM em-dashes are often spaced and mimic "punched up" sales writing, and that the sign is most useful together with other signs [E24]. Some vendors now suppress the em-dash, and a study cited by Wikipedia found in 2026-07 that among current models only Claude used em-dashes more than professional writers [E25]. Since product-ui runs on Claude, the em-dash signal stays relevant to its own output (lead's inference). In Japanese text, a Zenn author names asides set between full-width dashes as a habit of ChatGPT [E124].

Invented proof is named by 4 publishers [E8, E33, E107, E115]. Landing Doctor lists "invented testimonials, placeholder logos still sitting in the bar, or a precise stat with no source", with "trusted by 10,000 teams" and "increase conversions 312%" as examples [E33]. Capital and Compute names "invented metrics" [E8], the anti-ai-slop skill bans fabricated precision [E107], and a Japanese author lists 「架空のユーザーコメント」 among the signs of a thin page [E115].

Japanese sources add traits of their own, each from 1 or 2 authors [E113, E118, E120, E123, E124]:

- buzzwords: 「革新的」「最先端」「ワンストップ」「シームレス」 [E113];
- the same CTA, 「今すぐ相談する」, in every section, and stock photos of handshakes and meetings [E120];
- broken mixed setting of Japanese and Latin type, 「日英混排で崩れるのはAI初稿の典型」 [E118], and 游ゴシック mixed with an arbitrary Latin face, which the same author says lowers legibility [E119];
- English labels such as 「LOGIN」 on screens generated from Japanese prompts [E123];
- residue of AI-written Japanese prose: colon-heavy bullets, leftover bold, emoji decoration, too many headings, and words such as 「これにより」, which the Zenn author says a regular expression can catch [E124].

A thesis on about 37,000 Japanese abstract pairs found that human-written Japanese uses a more diverse vocabulary than LLM output [E46].

Stylistic markers detect poorly on their own. A review of 40 studies finds "no single reliable marker" [E53]. GPT detectors misclassify non-native English writing as AI-generated [E48]. Paraphrasing cut DetectGPT's accuracy from 70.3% to 4.6% at a 1% false-positive rate [E49]. Five blinded raters reached 19% accuracy, no better than chance [E50]. Raters who use LLMs often for writing did far better: their majority vote misclassified 1 of 300 articles, and they relied heavily on lexical clues [E51, E52]. For product-ui this means a word or punctuation list works as a warning on the skill's own drafts, where a false positive costs a rewrite, and fails as a verdict on who wrote a page (lead's inference).

### 3. Structural and behavioural defects and what they cost

No source in this run measures how often AI-generated screens lack states or ship controls that do nothing. The cost evidence comes from general usability research. Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) states that well-designed empty states "help increase user confidence, improve system learnability, and help users get started with key tasks quickly" [E88]. NN/g also states that error messages "must contain copy to elaborate and assist the user with recovering from the error" [E89], and that teams focused on the ideal path treat errors as "a frustrating afterthought" [E90]. Baymard reports that in its checkout testing some participants abandoned the process "due solely to vague or generic error messages", and that 98% of sites in its benchmark use generic messages [E91].

Errors and broken behaviour cost credibility. Over 1,400 survey respondents in 2000 reported that a site loses credibility when it has "errors, technical problems, or distracting advertisements" [E69]. Stanford's credibility guidelines, built on research with over 4,500 people, state that typographical errors and broken links "hurt a site's credibility more than most people imagine" [E92]. NN/g's 2016 study observed that typos and broken links communicate "an overall lack of attention to detail" [E65]. Pantheon, a hosting vendor that gains from the finding, reports that nearly 3 in 4 of its 1,000 respondents misidentified a legitimate site as an AI-driven scam "simply because it was slow or glitchy" [E59].

Accessibility failures are common across the web as a whole. WebAIM's 2025 scan of 1 million home pages found low-contrast text on 79.1% and missing alternative text on 55.5% [E85], and 34.2% of form inputs had no proper label [E86]. These figures cover human and AI-made pages alike, and product-ui already measures contrast and labels at render time.

Two Japanese authors locate the AI look in missing content and missing decisions. One lists what readers conclude within seconds, 「何のサイトか分からない」「文章がそれっぽいだけで信用できない」「実物が見えない」, and defines the trait as 「意思決定の跡がないこと」: who the page is for, what it shows first, what it cut and why the order is what it is [E114]. The same author names abstract illustrations, a blurred dashboard, meaningless charts and invented user comments as the places where the product should be [E115]. Another writes that AI makes average design and designers make design with intent [E122].

One study points the other way for application screens. In a blind survey of 92 participants, AI-generated prototypes scored positively on usability and efficiency and neutral or negative on originality and innovation, and the authors conclude that generative tools "tend to reinforce visual and structural patterns" [E93].

### 4. Why the traits repel

The strongest evidence concerns attribution. Paintings made by AI were judged more positively on liking, beauty and worth when labelled human-made than when labelled AI-made [E70]. Raters who could not tell AI text from human text in a blind test favoured text labelled "Human Generated" by over 30%, also when the labels were swapped [E71]. 13 experiments with over 5,000 participants found that people who disclose AI use are trusted less, explained by lower perceived legitimacy [E72, E61]. In the same research, clients placed 20% less trust in graphic designers after an AI disclosure [E62]. An advertising experiment with 304 participants found that AI disclosure lowered trust in the ad and the organisation through persuasion knowledge, with a smaller positive path as well [E63]. Labels on messages changed their evaluation without changing their ranking [E76], and AI labels lowered belief in claims while having little effect on stated engagement [E77].

Perceived effort carries much of the penalty. In an experiment with 618 participants, an AI-generated label reduced perceived creator effort, while a human-made label did not differ from no label [E73]. Across 3 studies, consumers forgave AI-created misrepresentative ads less than human-created ones because the AI ads seemed to involve less effort, and a strong brand reputation buffered the effect [E74]. This matches the practitioners' reading that a trait signals "nobody made a decision" [E4, E114].

Hidden authorship removes much of the penalty. Non-expert readers identified AI poems with 46.6% accuracy, below chance, and rated them more favourably on rhythm and beauty [E75]. The penalty therefore depends on a cue that triggers attribution. Frequent LLM users detect AI text well and rely on lexical clues [E51, E52], so as readers learn the named traits, the traits act as such cues (lead's inference, likely). Trade surveys point the same way but carry weaker evidence. A Harris Poll study with the 4As found that 73% would be less likely to trust an ad they suspected was AI-made, with no sample size reported [E56]. A Klaviyo survey reported that more than 75% of European consumers had noticed low-quality AI content in the past 6 months [E57].

Homogenisation is measured for text and argued for web design. Users produced less semantically distinct ideas with ChatGPT than with another tool [E78]. Each added human essay contributed more new ideas than each added GPT-4 essay, across 2,200 essays, and prompt changes did not close the gap [E79]. Stories by GPT-4 and LLaMA-3 repeated plot elements across generations and across models [E80]. Co-writing with an instruction-tuned model reduced lexical and content diversity [E47]. For web pages, a 2026 preprint argues that frictionless vibe coding can homogenise designs, without measuring it [E81]. Sameness across many pages is what makes a default recognisable at all, which explains why a trait that was harmless in one human-made template becomes a tell at scale (lead's inference).

Typicality cuts the other way. Visual appeal of a home page is judged within about 50 ms [E67]. In a study of 1,530 participants, a page's prototypicality correlated strongly with its aesthetics, perceived usability and trustworthiness, and its direct effect on trustworthiness outweighed the indirect ones [E68]. A conventional section order and familiar components therefore help; the harm lies in defaults that carry no product-specific decision and in content that is generic or invented (lead's inference from [E68, E74]).

Deception has its own mechanism. Humans detect fake reviews no better than chance, and learning 4 cues, among them generic descriptions, lowered trust in reviews and purchase intentions [E83]. Machine classifiers detected generated fake reviews better than human raters [E84]. Invented testimonials and unsourced figures on a landing page belong to this class: the reader may miss them, but once one is noticed, the page loses trust as a whole (lead's inference from [E83, E74]).

The word "creepy" has little support as a mechanism for UI. The uncanny-valley effect is large for human-looking artificial entities, with a pooled Hedges g of 1.01 across 72 studies [E82]. The reported backlash against Coca-Cola's 2024 AI holiday ad, called "deeply uncanny" and "soulless", concerned a film of people and characters [E64]. No study in this run ties an uncanny effect to page layout or UI copy.

Against the hypotheses in the brief: H2 (effort and deception) and H4 (the penalty needs attribution) have grade A support [E73, E74, E75, E83]. H3 (the look is sameness, and bans create the next default) has grade A support for text and grade C support for UI [E79, E80, E10]. H1, the user's view, holds for the existence of a recognisable cluster [E1, E13]; its harm is supported through attribution and effort in general, and trait by trait it rests on opinion.

### 5. Existing countermeasures and the evidence that they work

Published rule sets fall into 3 groups: vendor prompts and skills, open-source checkers, and general interface guidelines [E95, E103, E101]. Anthropic's cookbook prompt tells the model to avoid "Overused font families (Inter, Roboto, Arial, system fonts)" and "Clichéd color schemes (particularly purple gradients on white backgrounds)" [E95]. Its only evidence is one qualitative sentence, that without the prompt Claude "often defaults to simplistic designs with white and purple backgrounds" [E97]. Anthropic's frontend-design skill names the second set of defaults, the card kit with "the same soft grey shadow (rgba(0,0,0,.1))", template chrome, and fade-and-slide-up entrances that "read as AI-generated" [E98, E99, E100].

Open-source checkers run without a model. Impeccable describes "62 deterministic rules" with "No LLM, no API key" [E103]; its CLI scans source directories with pattern rules and runs rendered rules such as contrast and tiny text when given a URL [E105]. The ux-skill linter documents 152 regex rules, each encoding "one tell of AI-generated UI", that exit non-zero on Critical and High findings for CI use [E106]. The anti-ai-slop skill has 9 hard rules: no unbranded purple or indigo, no flat Inter or Geist, no uniform radius and padding, no emoji icons, no `transition: all` or blanket fade-in, no benefit-speak, no fabricated precision, a broken template skeleton, and divergence across a set [E107, E108, E109]. Its validation is a corpus of 10 artifacts rebuilt and scored by its author [E110]. Vercel's web-interface guidelines forbid `transition: all` and removing the focus outline without a replacement, and contain no rules on the AI look [E101, E102].

No source in this run shows that any of these rule sets reduces attribution to AI or raises trust. UI-Bench ranks the visual quality of 10 text-to-app tools with over 4,000 expert judgements and does not measure whether output looks AI-made [E111]. The cookbook itself reports that the model drifts to a new default font [E96], and Capital and Compute reports that agents move to a second palette once the first is banned [E10].

Compared with these sets, product-ui already covers part of the ground. S2 matches the cookbook's typeface list and adds Space Grotesk, S3 catches blue-to-purple gradients, S6 and S7 catch a single radius and a single shadow alpha, S8 catches emoji used as icons, S9 counts em-dashes, S10 requires state design on forms, S11 and R12 catch nested enclosures, and R15 catches rows of equal tiles that carry digits. The rule sets above check several traits that product-ui does not: motion on every section, glass panels, template chrome, stock vocabulary, negative parallelism and fabricated precision [E107, E109, E100].

## Disagreements and limits

Opposing evidence weighed in the Answer:

- A trait proves a default nobody chose, and no source treats it as proof of AI authorship [E9, E3].
- shadcn/ui and Tailwind ship no purple default, so the colour comes from the model and from templates built on Tailwind UI [E16, E2].
- Bento grids and conventional page orders are mainstream human practice, and prototypicality raises trust [E18, E68].
- Blind readers often cannot tell AI output apart and sometimes prefer it [E75, E71]; blind raters judged AI prototypes usable [E93].
- Word and punctuation markers are unreliable alone, misfire on non-native and plain prose, fade once publicised, and are suppressed by some newer models [E53, E48, E42, E25].
- A flagged phrase can be human-written [E31], and Wikipedia's guide says its list is no ban on words [E27].

`gaps.json` lists no sub-question without passing evidence. The collectors reported these searches as empty or out of reach:

- No study measures how readers react to the named UI traits one at a time; the visual and copy traits rest on grade C sources.
- No source measures how often AI-generated screens lack states or ship dead controls; a blog figure attributed to NN/g had no primary source and was excluded.
- No study measures triads, hedging or formulaic openings, and no study covers UI or marketing copy.
- No source ties Japanese line breaking, BudouX or full-width and half-width mixing to the AI look, and no news article quoting posts on X was found.
- No classifier of AI-generated UI and no outside rating of any anti-slop rule set was found; the Design Arena leaderboard, v0 and shadcn guidance, and ESLint or stylelint rules were not searched or fetched.
- Tuch et al. 2012 was paywalled, and several items rest on abstracts only.

Self-reported figures: Impeccable's 62 rules and its star count, ux-skill's 152 rules and anti-ai-slop's 10-artifact validation come from their authors. The survey figures from the Harris Poll with the 4As, Klaviyo and Pantheon come from interested sponsors, and 2 of them report no sample size. The Japanese traits come from 4 note authors, 1 Zenn author and 1 guide, with the 千歳開発 author behind 6 items.

What would change the Answer: a controlled study that varies the named visual traits with content held constant and measures attribution, trust or conversion; evidence that readers outside the design and developer community notice the traits at all; or a measured rate of missing states and dead controls in AI-generated screens.
